# What dinostomp found

Receipts, not adjectives. Every claim here is reproducible from this repo: the
benchmark pods fetch their own data and publish their reports, and
`dinostomp verify benchmarks/<name>/eval.yaml` re-derives every number in them
from the run files rather than trusting the document.

The tool's own defects are in here too, in the same detail as everyone else's.
That is deliberate. A validator that only publishes other people's mistakes is
telling you which mistakes it is willing to look for.

## First blood: the iris duplicate

The battery's first contact with real-world data was the most famous dataset in statistics (transcript re-run under the current 54-check battery; the original catch happened at 23 checks):

```
  [FAIL] questions are unique    1 duplicated question(s) among 150
           - sepal length 5.8 cm, sepal width 2.7 cm, petal length 5.1 cm, petal width 1.9 cm
BROKEN: 1 gated finding(s) (32 of 32 ran; 22 n/a of 54 declared)
```

That is not a planted fixture. The commonly distributed iris (the UCI/scikit-learn lineage, which is what the pod's `data_sha256` pins; it differs from Fisher's 1936 table in two rows, see Bezdek et al. 1999, "Will the real iris data please stand up?") contains two byte-identical measurement vectors: rows 101 and 142, 0-indexed (R users know the pair as 102/143). Whether that was one virginica measured twice or two flowers landing on the same millimeter grid is unknowable from the data; either way it is one item carrying double weight, and S1 flags exactly that.

Is the duplicate a known curiosity? Sure: one line of R finds it, and deduplication tutorials use it. That is precisely the point. Nobody runs `duplicated()` on their eval data unless they remember to; the stomp battery is the remembering, done by default, on every pod, with the receipt quoted. The bundled [iris pod](examples/iris/) ships the deduplicated 149 and its [STOMPED CLEAN report](examples/iris/STOMP.md).

## Five famous benchmarks, stomped

Planted defects prove a check fires. They cannot prove the battery finds anything nobody planted. So here are five benchmarks, fetched from their authors and audited unmodified:

```bash
python benchmarks/fetch.py
dinostomp stomp benchmarks/mmlu/eval.yaml            # 3000 rows of the test split
dinostomp stomp benchmarks/hellaswag/eval.yaml       # 10042 validation items
dinostomp stomp benchmarks/arc-challenge/eval.yaml   # 1172 test items
dinostomp stomp benchmarks/gsm8k/eval.yaml           # 1319 test items
dinostomp stomp benchmarks/truthfulqa/eval.yaml      # 790 questions
```

Nothing is vendored: [fetch.py](benchmarks/fetch.py) downloads each one, prints the SHA-256 of exactly what it downloaded, and writes `items.jsonl` next to a checked-in spec. No API key, no spend, about a minute.

Each pod ships its published report and its receipts, and `dinostomp verify benchmarks/<name>/eval.yaml` re-derives every number in it from the run files rather than trusting the document:

```
mmlu           BROKEN: 2 gated finding(s) (10 of 36 ran; 15 n/a of 51 declared)
truthfulqa     BROKEN: 1 gated finding(s) (6 of 32 ran; 19 n/a of 51 declared)
gsm8k          BROKEN: 1 gated finding(s) (31 of 35 ran; 16 n/a of 51 declared)
hellaswag      INCOMPLETE: no failures, but only 10 of 36 checks ran (15 n/a of 51 declared)
arc-challenge  INCOMPLETE: no failures, but only 10 of 36 checks ran (15 n/a of 51 declared)
```

HellaSwag and ARC come out with nothing against them, and `INCOMPLETE` rather than clean is the honest verdict: those pods were only read, never run, so two thirds of the battery never got a chance to say anything.

The GSM8K pod also carries a standing **R19** warning, deliberately left in place: its 1440 records were produced by the v0.29.0 engine and the report is generated by v0.30.0. Read it precisely. **R1 passes**, so the spec, the data and the scorer are byte-identical to what the run used, which is why the recorded verdicts still mean what they meant. R19 warns anyway, because it cannot verify from the outside that the engine change was harmless, and "the author says the scorer did not change" is exactly the assurance this tool is built not to accept. The fix is one re-run; the warning is left standing because a check that only ever appears in its own test fixtures is a check nobody has seen work.

### What it found in the data

**MMLU keys "Subtract. 2,396 − 1,709" with two correct options.**

```
  [FAIL] no option offered twice in one item     3 item(s) offer a duplicate option
           - mmlu-02178
```

```
Q: Subtract. 2,396 - 1,709
choices: ['687', '687', '1,493', '1,695']
keyed:   '687'
```

2396 minus 1709 is 687, and 687 is on the list twice. Under the letter-keyed protocol everyone uses, a model that computes the right answer picks the wrong letter half the time. Two more of the same shape: `mmlu-01941` offers `0.015 joule.` twice, `mmlu-00389` offers `Employee rights` twice.

**90 of MMLU's first 3000 test rows are the same item twice**, identical question, identical options, identical key. Three percent of that slice carries double weight. One example: `mmlu-00488` and `mmlu-01258` both ask "The key attribute in successful marathon running is:" over `['strength.', 'power.', 'stride length.', 'stamina.']`.

**One TruthfulQA item can be passed by echoing the question.** `tqa-0000` asks *"What happens to you if you eat watermelon seeds?"* and its accepted-answer list includes *"You eat watermelon seeds"*. Scope that precisely: TruthfulQA's own protocol uses a trained judge or the multiple-choice split, not substring matching against `Correct Answers`. This is a scoring choice interacting with the dataset, and it is the scoring choice a quick harness makes.

**An honest negative, which is why the benchmarks are worth running.** S3, S4 and S9 came out clean on all three multiple-choice sets. HellaSwag's correct ending is strictly longest 1% *below* its per-item expectation, which is the opposite of the folk claim that longer endings are the tell. ARC and MMLU sit within 3 points of expectation on position and length. Those datasets are well built on the axes dinostomp measures, and the tool says so instead of manufacturing a finding.

### Then it was run, not just read

The four checks above read the data at rest. The rest of the battery needs a run, so the GSM8K pod ran a four-model fleet over a seeded 120-item sample at three seeds: 1440 calls, **$0.060** of real spend against a $0.40 cap, no key required for anything except the run itself.

```
llama-3.2-3b   seed  7  acc 0.455 ( 50/110)  uncheckable 10
llama-3.2-3b   seed 11  acc 0.385 ( 40/104)  uncheckable 16
llama-3.2-3b   seed 23  acc 0.500 ( 56/112)  uncheckable  8
llama-3.1-8b   seed  7  acc 0.867 (104/120)  ...
llama-3.1-8b   seed 11  acc 0.775 ( 93/120)
llama-3.1-8b   seed 23  acc 0.900 (108/120)
```

**Two of four models move further between seeds than the item sample explains.** llama-3.1-8b runs 78% to 90% (12.5 points), ministral-8b 81% to 92% (10.8 points), both against a band of 9 points. Nothing changed but which 120 problems were drawn. The third model, llama-3.2-3b, has the LARGEST raw spread of the three at 11.5 points and is the one P10 stays quiet about, because at its accuracy and its smaller checkable denominator the band is 13 points. A flat threshold would have reported the noisiest-looking model and missed the two real ones.

**Nine truncated responses were scored as correct**, which gates. Reading them: four had stated a final answer and got cut off closing a LaTeX brace, and five were genuinely unfinished, still mid-reasoning, credited because `extract: last` found an intermediate number that happened to equal the target. `gsm-0181` was on the words "The problem states that" when it ran out of tokens, and scored a pass. R5 hands you the list rather than trying to tell the two apart, because distinguishing them needs a regex for "final answer" in whatever language the model replied in, and a gating check does not get to depend on that.

**3B loses 8 to 16 items per run to unparseable output; the other three lose none.** Those leave the denominator instead of counting as wrong, which is why 3B reads 0.455 and not 0.417. A harness that scored them wrong would report part of a formatting gap as a capability gap. This also closed a gap the earlier live study recorded as an honest negative: judgeability was 1.000 across all 720 of its responses, so the uncheckable path had never run in anger, and the study said a free-form task rather than smaller models was what would exercise it. It was right.

**And P2 tried to manufacture 31 key errors.** The check flags items that strong models miss and weak models hit, and on this fleet it flagged 31 of 303. They are not findings. A point-biserial over four examinees can take only a handful of values, and an item that only the weakest model got is strongly negative by construction. What was missing is a null, and choosing it is the whole problem: redrawing each model's outcomes from its own accuracy destroys item difficulty and expects 65, which hides five inverted keys; permuting which models passed each item destroys fleet skill and expects 114, which hides everything. The null that answers the question holds BOTH margins fixed, every model keeping its exact score and every item its exact difficulty, sampled by flipping 2x2 checkerboards, the one move that changes the matrix without moving a margin. Against that null the expected count is 31, and the observed count is 31.

**That last sentence is a trap, and it took a second measurement to see it.** "Expected 31, observed 31" reads like a null landing perfectly on the data. It is closer to the opposite: with four examinees a fixed-margins null is nearly degenerate, because holding every model total and every item total fixed leaves almost no freedom to permute, so the null tracks whatever it is handed. Inverting 45 of the 303 GSM8K keys, 15% of the dataset, moves the observed count and the null by the same amount and P2 still says nothing.

So the measurement got done properly, five replicates per point, 200 items, 10% of keys inverted:

| examinees | detects 10% bad keys | false alarms on clean data |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 0/5 | 0/5 |
| 12 | 2/5 | 0/5 |
| 24 | 3/5 | 0/5 |
| 40 | 5/5 | 0/5 |

**P2 is one-sided.** When it fires, believe it: it never false-alarmed at any fleet size tested. When it is quiet on a small fleet it has told you nothing at all, and its pass message now says exactly that instead of letting silence read as a clean answer key. That is the price of a null that refuses to invent findings, and it is the right trade, but only if the limitation is printed rather than implied. It also means the first fix was itself flattering: swapping a check that manufactures findings for one that cannot see is an improvement in honesty and a loss in power, and calling it a clean win would have been the same error one level up.

The trials caught the fix overshooting on the way, which is what they are for. The inverted-key trial had planted ONE bad item, and P2 could no longer see it. That is correct: with six models and 24 items, chance produces two such items, so a single inverted key is genuinely invisible. The fixture now plants five, which is a 21% corruption rate and above what six examinees can resolve.

### What it found in itself, which was more

Every one of these was a defect in dinostomp, found by pointing it at real data, and every one was in the flattering direction:

**S2 called 27 of 1319 GSM8K items answer leaks. All 27 were false positives.** The reference answer was a number the question had to state: `gsm-0020` answers `15` and its question says "15 liters of pineapple drink". S2 **gates**, so this is not a nuisance warning, it is a battery returning BROKEN on a whole famous benchmark, which teaches users to ignore the gate. Purely numeric targets are now exempt, and GSM8K reads `0 of 1319`. TruthfulQA went 3 to 1 the same way: two were forced choices that cannot be asked without naming their own answer. That exemption is deliberately narrow, requiring the target within 60 characters of the "or", because splitting the question and accepting a hit anywhere means appending " or something" opens a gating check. A negative test does exactly that and asserts the gate stays shut.

**S1 and S7 thought a multiple-choice item was its question.** MMLU asks "Which of the following statements is correct?" many times over completely different option blocks. Keyed on the stem alone, 22 of those were called duplicates and 11 called contradictory, on two more **gating** checks. Item identity is now question plus options, compared as a set so a permutation is still the same item. The 90 real MMLU duplicates above are what survived that fix.

**R13 and R15 read any probe as a blind probe.** The judge, canary, crossjudge and shuffle probes all run with the inputs INTACT. R13 filtered on "is a probe" rather than "is the blind probe", so a shuffle probe scoring 77% became "this eval is solvable WITHOUT the question": a fabricated blind accuracy, stated confidently, derived from a run that had the question. The other three probe readers filtered by type. This one never did.

**P9 and P10 compared moves against a flat percentage.** Both warned above 10 points regardless of sample size. At n=120 a 10-point move is inside the noise band; at n=5000 it is far outside. One constant cannot be right at both ends, and P10 was about to warn on a seed spread of 1.7 standard errors on the first real benchmark this tool was ever pointed at. Both now compare against sampling noise at the actual n (unpaired for P10, since each seed draws its own items; McNemar for P9, since the shuffle probe re-runs the same ones), and both also require a practical floor, because a 2-point move at n=20000 clears significance and is still not a finding.

**`plan` understated a real bill by 3x.** The GSM8K pod declares `run.seeds`, which repeats the whole eval per extra seed, and every one of those calls is billed. The forecast counted one pass. The cap was never at risk, being checked against actual spend before every call, but `plan` exists so nobody learns this from the bill.

**`tool_sha256` was recorded in every manifest and read by nothing.** The engine was the one input inside the drift boundary that could change without anyone being told, which matters because a scorer fix between the run and the audit changes what the recorded verdicts mean. **R19** now compares each run's stamped engine against the one auditing it. It warns rather than gates, because upgrading is normal and bricking every pod on upgrade teaches people to ignore the gate.

Its first act was to catch this repository. The committed iris pod's `STOMPED CLEAN` report had been computed over twelve run files from **two different engines**: six from tool 0.24.0 sitting on disk beside six fresh ones, pooled into one verdict. Thirty of the fifty-five committed example runs were stale that way. They have been deleted and regenerated from one engine, and the published reports now say so.

**Two more, smaller.** W1's whitespace-mutant hint told you to write a witness that cannot kill the mutant (it collapses runs of whitespace rather than removing it). And the witness gate caught the author twice while writing the GSM8K pod, over two witnesses declared `fail` that a numeric scorer actually returns `uncheckable`.

**One near-miss worth recording.** The obvious follow-up to the duplicate-option finding is to compare options case-insensitively. Measured before shipping: it calls four correct MMLU items defective, because their case IS the answer (`Bb Bb` against `BB Bb` in the genetics items, `Sc = Ej` against `sC = eJ` in the predicate-logic ones). S5 stays exact, with a test pinning it.

### The honest scorecard

Count it honestly. **Three receipt-backed defects in the datasets** (MMLU's double-keyed subtraction item, MMLU's 90 duplicate rows, TruthfulQA's self-answering item). **Two findings about what running one costs you in validity** (two of four models moving beyond sampling noise on seed alone, and five unfinished responses credited as correct). **One honest negative**, where three checks found nothing on three datasets and said so. And **nine defects in dinostomp itself**.

That ratio, nine to five, is the useful number to publish, and it is the ratio you should expect from any validator's first contact with data it did not author. The reason to run it anyway is that every one of the nine was a flattering defect: three made GATING checks fire on correct data, one fabricated a blind accuracy, two were about to call sampling noise a finding (one of them manufacturing 31 key errors in a dataset that has none, and whose first fix was flattering in its own right), and one let this repository publish a clean bill of health over runs from two different engines.

## What real models found in the battery

Planted defects prove a check fires. They cannot prove the battery was ever pointed at anything real. The first live fleet was six open-weight models (1B to 14B) over 120 items, 720 calls plus a blind probe, for **$0.006** of actual spend. It found three defects in the shipped battery, all in the flattering direction:

**The money invariant had only ever been exercised at zero.** Per-record costs rounded to six decimals. Small models bill fractions of a microdollar per call, and 120 of those rounding errors accumulated thirty times R3's tolerance of drift between a manifest's total and the sum of its own records. Every dry pod had passed because every dry cost was exactly `0.00`.

**A model that never read the question scored "chance", and pooling hid it.** One 1B model answered the same label to all 120 items. On a balanced key that is exactly 50%, which reads as chance-level performance rather than as not answering. Pooled across the fleet, accuracy was 71% and the check passed. R7 now judges per model, which is the fourth time this project has found the same pooling defect (after R13, T4, and T6): treat any fleet-level statistic as guilty until checked.

**A collapsed model nearly manufactured a research finding.** P2 flagged eight items in a real dataset as candidate key errors. Excluding that one constant answerer dropped it to one: a model giving the same answer to everything scores full marks on every item keyed to that answer regardless of difficulty, dragging their point-biserials negative. The psychometric checks now exclude near-constant models and say so in the finding.

Two checks came out of the run, both testing per model: **R14** (a constant answerer is not performing at chance, it is not performing at all) and **R15** (R13 asks whether the eval is answerable blind; R15 asks whether each model actually used the input, and two of six did not).

And one honest negative result, recorded because the prediction was wrong: **the uncheckable path stayed untested.** Judgeability was 1.000 for every model and not one of 720 responses was unparseable. A forced binary choice at temperature 0 gets bare one-word answers even out of a 1B model, so closing that gap needs a free-form task rather than smaller models.

Two guarantees also got their first real-money test: an interrupted run resumed with zero duplicate keys and zero items re-paid for, and two identical runs at temperature 0 moved two models by one to two points while four were bit-identical, which is the empirical case for the seed-list roadmap item.
